Say one word for me. Any word, in Telugu.

*Amma.*

It came out without effort, didn't it? You didn't think about it. You never do. You read a bus board in Vijayawada, you bargain with a shopkeeper in Guntur, you fill a form in a government office, and the words simply work.

Why do they work?

Because seventy-four years ago, a man stopped eating so that they would.

---

To understand what he wanted, you have to see the map he was looking at.

In 1952, India had been free for five years. But freedom had not redrawn the lines the British left behind. The Telugu districts of the coast and Rayalaseema were part of Madras State, governed from Madras city. The Telangana districts were part of Hyderabad State. One people, one language, cut in two, and in both halves the language of power was not theirs.

Imagine a farmer from Ongole travelling to Madras over a land dispute. The petition is in a language he can't read. The officer across the desk speaks another. He nods at things he doesn't understand, signs where he is told, and goes home without knowing what he agreed to.

That was not a rare day. For millions of people, that was government.

And it wasn't only offices. A child in a Telugu village could finish school and find that the next door, the college, the exam, the job, opened in another language. A mother could stand in a hospital corridor in Madras and not know how to ask what was wrong with her son. Nobody was cruel to them, exactly. The system simply wasn't built for them to understand it.

That kind of quiet exclusion doesn't make headlines. It just makes people smaller, one form at a time.

The demand for a Telugu state was not new. It had been made for decades. Committees were formed. Reports were written. Promises were made in speeches and forgotten in files.

Around the time of independence, the question of states drawn by language came up again and again. Each time, it was put off. There were bigger fears in the air: Partition had just torn the country apart, and many leaders worried that dividing provinces by language would divide India itself. Their fear was real. So was the waiting.

So what does a people do when asking politely has failed for decades?

---

The man who answered was not a politician.

Potti Sriramulu was born in Madras on 16 March 1901, into a Telugu family. He studied sanitary engineering in Bombay and took a job with the Great Indian Peninsula Railway. A steady salary. A respectable life. The kind of life most of us are still working towards.

Then, in 1928, his wife died in childbirth. A few days later, their baby died too. He was 27.

Some losses make a person smaller. This one emptied him, and he filled the space with something else. After his mother died, he left the railway in 1930 and went to Gandhi at the Sabarmati Ashram. He marched in the Salt Satyagraha. He went to jail. In Nellore he fasted until the Venugopala Swami temple opened its doors to Dalits, who had been kept outside for generations.

Gandhi is said to have remarked that with eleven more followers like Sriramulu, he could win freedom in a year.

He was not loud. He did not give fiery speeches. He was stubborn the way water is stubborn: it doesn't argue with the stone, it simply keeps going until the stone gives way.

Why would a man like that starve himself for a line on a map?

Because to him, it was never a line on a map. It was the farmer at the desk. It was the mother in the corridor. It was a whole people being told, politely and for years, to wait.

He had already learned what most of us spend our lives avoiding: that death is certain, and the only question is what you spend your life on before it comes. He had buried his wife and his child. He was not afraid of the ending. He was afraid of a life that changed nothing.

---

On 19 October 1952, he sat down in a house in Mylapore, Madras. It belonged to Bulusu Sambamurthy, a freedom fighter who had offered it to him. The address was 126 Royapettah High Road.

He stopped eating.

There was no announcement on the radio that he would succeed. There was no crowd outside on the first day. There was a man, a room, and a decision.

The first days of a fast are about hunger. Then the hunger fades, and the body begins to live on itself. The weight goes first. Then the strength. Standing becomes difficult. Then sitting. Then even speaking costs something.

Why didn't anyone stop it at once? Because many people believed it would end the way such fasts often ended: with a promise, a glass of juice, a photograph. He had fasted before, and the temple in Nellore had opened. Surely this would be the same.

It wasn't the same. This time, the people he was asking were in Delhi, and Delhi was far away.

Day ten.

Day twenty.

Day thirty.

People came to the house. Some came to bless him. Some came to beg him to stop. Messages passed between Madras and Delhi, and in Delhi the government waited, perhaps believing he would give in, the way most of us give in when the body begins to fail.

As the weeks went on, the fast stopped being news from Madras and became something people carried home. In the Telugu districts, they followed it the way you follow a relative in hospital. What day is it now? How is he? Is there any word from Delhi?

Day forty.

Day fifty.

By then, he could barely lift his head. The body had nothing left to give. Only the decision was still standing.

He did not give in.

On 15 December 1952, on the fifty-eighth day, Potti Sriramulu died. He was 51 years old.

---

Fifty-eight days. Count them on your own calendar sometime. It is the time between one festival and the next. It is two monthly salaries. It is long enough for a seed to become a plant.

It was long enough for one man to end.

What happened next happened fast.

The news ran through the Telugu districts like fire through dry grass. Towns shut down. Rioting broke out, and in Anakapalle and Vijayawada police opened fire. Seven people were killed.

On 19 December, four days after his death, Prime Minister Nehru announced that a separate Andhra State would be formed. It came into being on 1 October 1953, with Kurnool as its capital: the first state of independent India formed around a language.

Then the question he had forced travelled across the whole country. If Telugu people deserved a state of their own, why not every language? In 1956, India redrew its internal map along the lines of language. On **1 November 1956**, Andhra State and the Telugu districts of Hyderabad State were joined to form Andhra Pradesh.

Even now, the date is argued over. After Telangana became a state of its own on 2 June 2014, Andhra Pradesh marked 2 June instead of 1 November, from 2014 to 2018. In 2019, a new government brought the celebration back to 1 November. The state also observes 15 December, the day he died, as Atmarpana Dinam, the Day of Sacrifice. Governments change. Dates move. The man does not.

His home district carries his name now. In 2008, Nellore became Sri Potti Sriramulu Nellore. People call him *Amarajeevi*: the one who lives forever.

But the truest memorial isn't a statue, or the name on a district.

It's you.

A little girl in Kakinada says her first word in Telugu. You send your mother a voice note in the language she sang you to sleep in. Both of you are living inside a decision a man made in a borrowed room in Mylapore, seventy-four years ago.

He never asked us to fast. He never asked us for anything.

But a language lives only as long as someone is speaking it.

He gave his breath so that ours could carry these words.

*మాట మనది. మనిషి ఆయన.*
*The words are ours. The man was him.*
